かな Kana

Japanese has two syllabaries, hiragana and katakana, with the same sounds. Both grew out of kanji used for their sound: hiragana are cursive forms of a whole character, katakana keep one piece of it. Pick a kana to see where it came from.

Where does a kana's sound come from?

Japanese had no writing until it borrowed Chinese characters. To spell Japanese words, scribes (by the 8th century, in the Man'yōshū poetry anthology) used kanji purely for their sound and ignored the meaning — so 安 "peaceful" just stood for a. These sound-only kanji are called man'yōgana; kana are simplified forms of them.

Most were picked for their Chinese-derived reading (on'yomi): Japan's approximation of how Chinese sounded in the 5th–8th centuries. Readings longer than one syllable were cut down: 安 an → あ a, 末 matsu → ま ma. A few used a native Japanese word written with the kanji (kun'yomi): 女 "woman" is Japanese me, giving め.

For comparison each kanji also shows a reconstruction of Tang-dynasty Chinese (around the time kana formed) and modern Mandarin, which has drifted much further: 波 was *bɑ, is bō today, and gave Japanese ha (then pronounced pa).
